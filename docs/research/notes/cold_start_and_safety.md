# Cold Start, Demo Validation, and Minimum Trust & Safety for "Niches"

Scope: launch and cold-start tactics for a new hobby/niche social app (Cooking, Art, Photography, Gym), how to validate a demo with real users, and the minimum moderation, safety and legal setup for a US-based solo founder. Researched November 2026. Note: `support.google.com` and `platform.openai.com` could not be fetched from this research session (the network blocked them), so Google Play and OpenAI details below come from search snippets or secondary pages and should be checked against the primary pages.

## Q1. Cold start: which launch tactics fit a hobby/niche app?

### Takeaway
Every successful precedent began with one small, tightly defined, hand-recruited group of high-quality creators (the "hard side"), and those creators were often given scarcity or status: design bloggers on Pinterest, designers and photographers on Instagram, local cycling clubs on Strava, paid creators on Lemon8, campus ambassadors at BeReal and Fizz. For Niches, the evidence points to launching one niche first (or one niche within one community) with 20 to 50 hand-recruited creators who post before regular users arrive, not four empty feeds at once.

### Cited Findings
**Andrew Chen's framework**
- Chen's core idea is the "atomic network": the smallest network with enough people that everyone keeps coming back. Successful networked products usually started in niche markets. — [Twosapp/Medium summary](https://medium.com/twosapp/the-cold-start-problem-how-to-start-and-scale-network-effects-by-andrew-chen-813f0668c70f); [Antoine Buteau summary](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/the-cold-start-problem-book-summary.md)
- Every network has a "hard side": the scarcer group whose participation creates outsized value. On a content network that is the creators, compared with the "easy side" of casual consumers. Chen's examples include women on Tinder and employers on LinkedIn. His advice for winning that side is to "solve a hard problem" for them. — [Twosapp/Medium summary](https://medium.com/twosapp/the-cold-start-problem-how-to-start-and-scale-network-effects-by-andrew-chen-813f0668c70f)
- Chen points to hobbies and side hustles as a source of networks, because what people do on nights and weekends is underused time and energy. That fits Niches' hobby focus directly. — [search summary of Chen's book](https://medium.com/twosapp/the-cold-start-problem-how-to-start-and-scale-network-effects-by-andrew-chen-813f0668c70f)
- The book describes five stages: Cold Start, Tipping Point, Escape Velocity, Hitting the Ceiling, and Moat. The cold start has to be solved again for each new network, which for Niches means each new niche. — [Charter / Time summary](https://www.charterworks.com/the-cold-start-problem-andrew-chen/)
- Caveat: these are secondary summaries, not Chen's own text.

**Pinterest (hobby and curation precedent, closest analogue)**
- After the January 2010 Alt Summit, Ben Silbermann worked with about 30 design bloggers on "Pin It Forward", a blog campaign promoting Pinterest. The site was invite-only, and those bloggers could hand out invites. — [Product Habits](https://producthabits.com/how-pinterest-became-an-11-billion-company-by-organizing-the-worlds-hobbies/); [Alexander Jarvis, "Pinterest doing things that don't scale"](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/pinterest-doing-things-that-dont-scale)
- Silbermann personally recruited many of the early invitees. Growth was slow: about 10,000 users nine months after launch. — [Innovators Uncensored](https://innovatorsuncensored.beehiiv.com/p/how-pinterest-got-its-first-users); [Product Habits](https://producthabits.com/how-pinterest-became-an-11-billion-company-by-organizing-the-worlds-hobbies/)

**Instagram**
- According to notes on Sarah Frier's *No Filter*, the founders picked their first users carefully. They courted people likely to be good photographers, especially designers with large Twitter followings, who then became evangelists. — [Goodreads notes on No Filter](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/52194898-no-filter/57884648-jos-garc-a-herz/9c25de7b-30e1-44ae-a145-50ef4bc990e2) (secondary; check against the book)

**Reddit (seeding with fake accounts)**
- Steve Huffman said in a Udacity video that he and Alexis Ohanian ran many fake accounts in Reddit's early days. They submitted links, then upvoted and commented from other accounts, to make the site look busy and set its tone. Ohanian said many early "users" were the two of them using aliases. The need for fake accounts faded as real users arrived. — [Vice/Motherboard](https://www.vice.com/en/article/how-reddit-got-huge-tons-of-fake-accounts-2/); [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/the-secret-to-reddits-early-growth-fake-accounts/); [Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/steve-huffman-built-reddit-fake-accounts)

**Strava (hobby, community, progress-tracking precedent)**
- Strava was founded in San Francisco in 2009 by Mark Gainey and Michael Horvath. Gainey said the team went to local cycling clubs to find early customers. Growth turned exponential in 2011, when the mobile app made Strava usable by anyone with a smartphone. — [Stanford eCorner transcript "Strava finds its niche"](https://stvp.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/09/strava-finds-its-niche-transcript.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strava); [Alexander Jarvis](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/strava-doing-things-that-dont-scale)

**BeReal (campus ambassadors)**
- BeReal paid college ambassadors to grow its campus presence by hosting parties that encouraged downloads and by finding campus events where BeReal could appear. The Brown Daily Herald reported a $30-per-person referral bonus. — [Brown Daily Herald, Feb 2022](https://www.browndailyherald.com/article/2022/02/new-social-media-app-tells-students-to-bereal); [BestColleges](https://bestcolleges.com/news/2022/04/22/social-network-bereal-college-students/)
- A two-week NYC campaign run by the agency Home From College reportedly produced 3,873 sign-ups, 301% above its goal. This figure is published by the vendor. — [Home From College case study](https://homefromcollege.com/blog/case-studies/bereal)
- TechCrunch later reported that at least some of BeReal's growth was manufactured to make it look like a viral hit. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2305292)
- BeReal was still hiring an "Offline Growth Manager USA" in 2025 to run school and university ambassador programs. — [Lever job posting](https://jobs.lever.co/voodoo/4d0e0113-ce09-4866-898e-cea5a17f721c)

**Fizz**
- A secondhand account says Fizz ambassadors on Penn's campus slipped small paper notes explaining Fizz under doors and onto tables in college buildings. — [Allen Yao, "The consumer app growth playbook" (Substack)](https://allenyaoo.substack.com/p/the-consumer-app-growth-playbook)

**Lemon8 (paid creators, plus a cautionary outcome)**
- Business Insider and The New York Times reported that Lemon8 paid creators and offered other incentives in the US and UK to post. Creators were brought into a launch cohort and asked to post throughout April 2023 before regular users were let in from May. — [TechCrunch, Mar 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/28/tiktok-ban-backup-plan-bytedance-owned-instagram-rival-lemon8-hits-the-u-s-app-stores-top-10/%20); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/TikTok-Owner-Boosts-New-Product-Focused-App/646335/)
- ByteDance also reportedly paid creators to promote Lemon8 on TikTok, often without sponsorship labels. A ByteDance source denied this. — [TechCrunch, Aug 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/11/bytedance-again-looks-to-tiktok-to-promote-its-other-social-app-lemon8)
- Lemon8 reached the US App Store Top 10 in March 2023, but by November 2023 TechCrunch reported it had "failed to gain traction" in the US, with 2.6M total US downloads. — [TechCrunch, Nov 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/03/lemon8-the-instagram-rival-from-tiktok-parent-bytedance-fails-to-gain-traction-in-u-s)

**Cara (values-driven niche, plus a scaling risk)**
- Cara, an artist portfolio and social app with an anti-AI stance, launched in January 2023. In June 2024 it grew from about 40,000 to about 650,000 users in a week, driven by artists' anger that Meta was using public Instagram and Facebook posts to train AI. The surge crashed the app. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2790719); [Entrepreneur](https://entrepreneur.com/business-news/anti-ai-app-booming-in-popularity-as-artists-leave-instagram/475255); [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/anti-ai-art-portfolio-platform-cara-reaches-300-000-users/)

### Inferences
- **One niche first.** Chen's atomic-network logic and every precedent above (Pinterest with design bloggers, Strava with cycling clubs, Instagram with designers) point to launching one niche, ideally one niche in one physical community, such as Gym at one university or Cooking among one friend group or one club. Four sparse feeds are worse than one dense feed. The other niches can sit in the UI as "coming soon" or waitlisted.
- **The hard side is creators who post progress.** Recruit 20 to 50 people who already post their hobby, for example campus lifting clubs, culinary students, art and photo club members, or small TikTok and Instagram hobby creators. Have them post for one to two weeks before opening to friends, as Lemon8 did with its creator cohort but at a much smaller scale.
- **Seeding content.** A founder-run "house" account that posts or curates openly (prompts, weekly challenges, "show your first loaf") avoids the trust and legal risk of Reddit-style fake personas. Undisclosed fake users or unlabeled paid promotion carry FTC endorsement-disclosure risk and Gen Z backlash risk; the Lemon8 and BeReal coverage shows journalists look for this. This is my inference; the FTC endorsement rules were not researched here.
- **Campus launches** suit a Gen Z app because density is achievable in one place. Ambassadors with small cash or status rewards (BeReal's $30 referral, Fizz's flyers) are cheap at demo scale.
- **Waitlists and invite-only access** (Pinterest) create scarcity and status and let you control density. **TestFlight** fits the demo stage for iOS (see Gaps for its limits).
- **The lesson of Lemon8 and Cara:** paid or event-driven spikes do not equal retention. Cara's crash shows that infrastructure and moderation have to be ready for a spike.
- **A wedge or values hook** (Cara's anti-AI stance; Niches' hidden likes and chronological feed as "anti-algorithm") can be a launch narrative, but it has to be credible and consistently enforced.

## Q2. Validating a demo: interviews, metrics, benchmarks, sample size

### Takeaway
Use Mom Test-style interviews (ask about past behavior, don't pitch) with roughly 5 users per round of usability testing, repeated across several rounds. Then run a small closed beta and judge it on cohort retention, with a16z's social-app tiers as the bar (OK D1/D7/D30 = 50/35/20%, Good = 60/40/25%, Great = 70/50/30%), plus creation metrics such as the share of users who post weekly.

### Cited Findings
**The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)**
- The three rules: (1) talk about the customer's life, not your idea, so don't pitch; (2) ask about specifics in the past, not generalities or hypotheticals about the future; (3) talk less and listen more. Compliments and hypothetical "I would use that" answers are unreliable; facts and past behavior count. — [mtlynch.io book report](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/); [Unusual Ventures](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)
- The name comes from the idea that the questions are good enough that even your mom, who wants to protect your feelings, can't mislead you. — [Medium / The Wisdom Project](https://medium.com/the-wisdom-project/ask-questions-even-your-mom-cant-lie-to-rob-fitzpatrick-the-wisdom-project-dbe6608f88df)
- A secondary guide suggests the interviewer should talk no more than about 20% of the time. That is a heuristic, not a rule from the book. — [Koji](https://www.koji.so/docs/mom-test-user-interviews)

**Usability sample size**
- NN/g (Jakob Nielsen): testing with 5 users finds about 85% of usability problems. Several small rounds (test 5, fix, retest) beat one large study. About 15 users are needed to find nearly all problems, and the rule assumes comparable users. With distinct user groups, such as gym users and artists, test each group separately. — [NN/g, "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users"](https://nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)
- Critics argue the formula's assumptions can fail for some tests. — [CORE: "Why and when five test users aren't enough"](https://core.ac.uk/works/16907959)

**Retention benchmarks for social apps**
- a16z (Bryan Kim, 2023), "How to Benchmark Your Social App": OK = D1 50%, D7 35%, D30 20%; Good = D1 60%, D7 40%, D30 25%; Great = D1 70%, D7 50%, D30 30%. — [a16z](https://a16z.com/do-you-have-lightning-in-a-bottle-how-to-benchmark-your-social-app/)
- A figure often attributed to Andrew Chen, "60/30/15 is very good", appears only in a secondary blog. I could not verify it against Chen's own writing. — [via search results; unverified](https://www.plotline.so/blog/retention-rates-mobile-apps-by-industry)
- Medians across all apps are far lower: about 27% D1, 9% D7, 4% D30 per MWM (2026), and social/messaging at roughly D1 25–29%, D7 9–10%, D30 about 5% per Lovable. Other 2026 guides put social at 48/28/14. These vendor numbers disagree widely because their methods and samples differ. — [MWM](https://mwm.ai/glossary/n-day-retention); [Lovable](https://lovable.dev/guides/what-is-a-good-retention-rate-for-an-app); [Unstar](https://unstar.app/blog/app-retention-benchmarks-2026)
- Definition matters. "N-day" retention (returned on exactly day N) is lower than "rolling" retention (returned on day N or later), so compare like with like. — [MWM](https://mwm.ai/glossary/n-day-retention)
- Andrew Chen's older posts show that losing most mobile users early is normal; the post title is "New data shows why losing 80% of your mobile users is normal". — [andrewchen.com](https://andrewchen.com/new-data-shows-why-losing-80-of-your-mobile-users-is-normal/)

### Inferences
- **Demo validation sequence:**
  1. 10–20 Mom Test interviews per niche about current behavior: where they post their cooking, art or gym progress today, what they hate about Instagram and TikTok, and whether they keep progress logs.
  2. 2–3 rounds of 5-user usability tests on the clickable demo or TestFlight build.
  3. A closed beta with one niche cohort of about 30–100 people, tracked for 4+ weeks.
- **Metrics to watch in the beta:**
  - D1, D7 and D30 retention by cohort, against the a16z tiers.
  - Weekly active posters as a share of weekly actives. Use the creator ratio as the north-star for a content network.
  - Posts per active user per week.
  - Time to first post.
  - Share of users who set a goal or post a progress update.
  - Saves per user (a substitute engagement signal, since likes are hidden).
  - Share of posts that get at least one comment within 24 hours. Creators who get no response churn.
- **Sample-size caution:** with 30–100 beta users, retention percentages are noisy (one person is 1–3 points). Treat them as directional and focus on qualitative "why" plus behavior such as unprompted repeat posting.
- **Hidden likes** take away the usual dopamine loop, so comments, saves and progress milestones have to replace that feedback. That is worth testing explicitly in interviews.

## Q3. Minimum trust & safety and moderation tooling

### Takeaway
To get onto the App Store, a UGC app must have content filtering, in-app reporting with timely responses, user blocking, and published contact information (Apple Guideline 1.2), plus in-app account deletion (5.1.1(v)). On Google Play a social app must also publish child sexual abuse and exploitation (CSAE) standards, provide a feedback mechanism, and name a child-safety point of contact. Federally, a US provider must report CSAM it becomes aware of to NCMEC and preserve it for 1 year (REPORT Act 2024), but it is not required to scan. Cheap tooling exists: OpenAI's moderation endpoint (reported free; multimodal since Sept 2024), AWS Rekognition at about $0.001 per image, and Cloudflare's free CSAM Scanning Tool.

### Cited Findings
**Apple App Store Guideline 1.2 (verbatim, current page fetched Nov 2026)**
- "Apps that enable users to create, generate, upload, share, or otherwise contribute content... must include: A method for filtering objectionable material from being posted to the app; A mechanism to report offensive content and timely responses to concerns; The ability to block abusive users from the service; Published contact information so users can easily reach you." — [Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apps "used primarily for pornographic content, Chatroulette-style experiences, random or anonymous chat, objectification of real people (such as 'hot-or-not' voting), making physical threats, or bullying do not belong on the App Store." Developers who fail to fix violations after notice may be removed. — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 1.2.1(a) (Creator Content): apps must let users identify content that exceeds the app's age rating and must "use an age restriction mechanism based on verified or declared age to limit access by underage users." — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 5.1.1(v): an app that supports account creation "must also offer account deletion within the app." — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 2.3.6: answer the age rating questions honestly; a mis-rated app "could trigger an inquiry from government regulators." — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

**Google Play**
- Child Safety Standards policy: apps in the Social and Dating categories must (1) publish globally accessible web standards that explicitly prohibit CSAE, (2) name a child-safety point of contact in Play Console, "an individual who is ready and able to speak to your organization's CSAM prevention practices", and (3) self-certify compliance. Scope does not depend on whether children use the app. Full compliance was required by March 19, 2025. — [Google Play Console Help 14747720](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14747720) (via search snippet; direct fetch blocked)
- Google Play's UGC policy (answer 9876937) could not be fetched. From memory and unverified: it requires users to accept terms of service or a content policy before creating content, robust and ongoing moderation, an in-app way to report content and users, and an in-app way to block users. — [Google Play UGC policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9876937)

**CSAM obligations (US)**
- 18 U.S.C. § 2258A requires providers to report apparent CSAM they become aware of to NCMEC's CyberTipline. The REPORT Act (Pub. L. 118-59, 2024) extended the required preservation of reported content from 90 days to 1 year, allows voluntary longer preservation, and sets preservation-method requirements. — [Pub. L. 118-59, congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/118/plaws/publ59/PLAW-118publ59.htm)
- § 2258A does not require providers to monitor or scan all user content. This comes from a secondary summary; the statute's subsection (f) is the primary text. — [policyrisk.com 18 USC 2258A](https://policyrisk.com/us-code/title/18/section/2258A)
- Cloudflare's CSAM Scanning Tool is free. It uses fuzzy hashing against NCMEC hash lists and no longer requires NCMEC credentials, only a verified email. On a match it blocks the image (HTTP 451) and notifies the customer. Cloudflare reports matches to NCMEC, but site operators "are still expected" to file their own reports. Adoption reportedly rose 1,600% after the credential requirement was dropped. It only scans content served through Cloudflare's cache. — [Cloudflare blog, update](https://blog.cloudflare.com/a-simpler-path-to-a-safer-internet-an-update-to-our-csam-scanning-tool/); [Cloudflare blog, original](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-csam-scanning-tool); [INHOPE](https://inhope.org/EN/articles/cloudflare-simplifying-the-fight-against-csam)

**Automated moderation tools and costs**
- OpenAI `omni-moderation-latest` (announced Sept 26, 2024) handles text and images. Image (or image plus text) classification covers six categories; the others are text-only. — [OpenAI announcement](https://openai.com/index/upgrading-the-moderation-api-with-our-new-multimodal-moderation-model/)
- OpenAI's moderation endpoint is described as free by third-party pages; I could not confirm this on OpenAI's pricing or docs page because of the fetch block. — [CometAPI](https://www.cometapi.com/pl/models/openai/omni-moderation-latest/); [Eden AI comparison](https://www.edenai.co/post/content-moderation-apis-text-image-and-video-compared)
- AWS Rekognition content moderation costs about $0.001 per image (about $1 per 1,000) and $0.10 per minute of video by default, per an AWS blog. — [AWS ML blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-to-decide-between-amazon-rekognition-image-and-video-api-for-video-moderation/)
- Hive: a third-party comparison lists about $1.50 per 1K text requests, $3.00 per 1K images and $0.13 per video minute. Unconfirmed; no official Hive pricing page was found. — [WaveSpeed comparison 2026](https://wavespeed.ai/blog/posts/best-ai-content-moderation-apis-tools-2026)
- Google Cloud image moderation (Vision SafeSearch) is listed at about $1.00 per 1,000 images in a third-party comparison. — [Eden AI](https://www.edenai.co/post/top-10-explicit-content-detection-apis)

**EU and UK notes**
- UK Online Safety Act: all in-scope user-to-user services, "large or small", must comply with illegal-content duties. That includes an illegal content risk assessment (due by 16 March 2025 for existing services), and the codes applied from 17 March 2025. Ofcom offers a compliance tool for services. — [Ofcom guide for services](https://ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/guide-for-services); [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/ofcom-publishes-final-illegal-content-risk-assessment-guidance-and-codes-practice-under-uk)

### Inferences (minimum T&S stack for a demo or early launch)
- **Must-have before any public App Store or Play listing:**
  - Report button on every post, comment and profile.
  - Block user.
  - Filtering at upload: run text and images through the OpenAI moderation endpoint, or Rekognition (about $1 per 1,000 images, a negligible cost at demo scale).
  - A published support email and contact page.
  - In-app account deletion.
  - Terms of service and community guidelines users accept at signup, including explicit CSAE prohibition language (Google requirement).
  - A 13+ age gate (see Q4).
  - Founder-reviewed report queue with a stated response target, for example within 24 hours.
- **CSAM:** route media through Cloudflare (free CSAM Scanning Tool) or similar hash matching. Register with the NCMEC CyberTipline so you can file reports, and keep a written internal procedure: do not view or redistribute, preserve for 1 year, report, ban the account. Never download or forward suspected material, even to a lawyer.
- **Product choices that reduce risk:** no DMs at first (DMs are where grooming and harassment cluster); no anonymous posting; private-by-default for under-18 accounts if minors are allowed; hidden likes and a chronological feed already reduce some comparison pressure. Note that Apple bans "hot-or-not" style rating of people, so avoid any feature that rates bodies, which matters for Gym.
- **Video** is the costliest modality to moderate. Consider launching photo plus text only, or capping video length.
- **For a TestFlight-only demo** with invited testers, the full stack is less critical, but building report and block early is cheap and needed for App Review anyway.

## Q4. Legal basics for a US solo founder, plus niche-specific risks

### Takeaway
Minimum legal kit:
- A privacy policy that matches actual data collection.
- Terms of service with a content license and moderation rights.
- Community guidelines.
- A 13+ age gate (COPPA; the amended rule has been fully in force since April 22, 2026).
- A registered DMCA agent with the US Copyright Office ($6, renewed every 3 years) to keep the copyright safe harbor. Section 230 does not cover IP or federal crimes, which is why the DMCA registration matters.

Niche risks: gym content can spread eating disorder and body-image harm, and hashtag bans alone are known to fail. Art and photo communities need a clear stance on AI-generated work and on reposting others' work.

### Cited Findings
**COPPA**
- The FTC's amended COPPA Rule was published April 22, 2025, took effect June 23, 2025, and required full compliance by April 22, 2026. — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/coppa-rule-amendment-compliance-deadline-approaches); [Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know.html)
- A February 25, 2026 FTC policy statement limits COPPA enforcement against general- and mixed-audience services that collect children's data solely to verify age without prior parental consent. — [Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know.html)

**DMCA designated agent**
- The Copyright Office's electronic system charges a flat $6 per registration, amendment or renewal. A designation expires after 3 years unless renewed or amended. Registration is required to keep the § 512 safe harbor. — [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/copyright-office-establishes-new-electronic-dmca-agent-registration); [Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/ar/insights/2016/12/new-dmca-regulations-existing-new-designated-agents-must-register-online) (2016–17 sources; confirm the current fee at copyright.gov/dmca-directory)

**Section 230**
- Section 230 generally shields platforms from liability for user content. It expressly excludes federal criminal law (§230(e)(1)), intellectual property (§230(e)(2)), electronic communications privacy law (§230(e)(4)) and, since FOSTA-SESTA in 2018, sex-trafficking laws. Courts are split on state IP claims such as right of publicity. — [CRS, "Section 230: An Overview"](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2024-01-04_R46751_9fa55d4913ef4d0ce4a81cb34dedf3333782a277.html); [Farella Braun + Martel](https://www.fbm.com/post/102ks40/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_230)

**Gym, body image and eating disorders**
- Sharp & Gerrard (2022, *Body Image*) argue that Instagram's pro-ED hashtag moderation and hidden like counts are both "quick technical fixes" that don't address underlying harm. They cite the leaked internal Instagram research from September 2021 on teen girls' body image. — [Western Sydney University research record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/the-body-image-problem-on-social-media-novel-directions-for-the-f/)
- Gerrard (2018, *New Media & Society*) found users evade hashtag bans with untagged content and coded signals, and recommendation systems recirculate pro-ED content. ### Inferences
- **Documents.** For a demo, generate the privacy policy and terms from a reputable generator such as Termly or iubenda, then have a lawyer review before public launch. Make sure the privacy policy matches what the app and any SDKs actually collect (analytics, Supabase or Firebase, crash reporting), and that it matches Apple's privacy "nutrition label" and Google's Data safety form.
- **Age.** Set a hard minimum age of 13+ with a neutral date-of-birth gate (don't hint at the right answer). Delete accounts that self-identify as under 13. Consider 16+ or 18+ for the beta to sidestep minors' issues entirely; a Gen Z college launch makes 18+ easy. State laws on minors and social media (for example Utah, Texas, Florida) and Apple's and Google's age-signal requirements are moving targets and were not researched here.
- **DMCA and Section 230.** Register the DMCA agent ($6) as soon as there is public UGC; it is cheap and needed because Section 230 does not cover copyright claims. Publish a takedown process (form or email) and a repeat-infringer policy.
- **Gym policy.** Prohibit pro-ED content, extreme calorie-restriction coaching, steroid and drug sourcing, and body-rating comments. Discourage weight numbers in progress posts by default, or let users hide them, and add an option to hide before/after photos. Show eating-disorder helpline resources (for example NEDA or the 988 Lifeline) for flagged search terms. The research shows hashtag blocklists get evaded, so pair them with report categories, human review, and design choices: no body-rating, hidden likes, no "most improved body" leaderboards.
- **Cooking policy.** Food-safety misinformation (unsafe canning, raw-meat practices, dangerous "hacks") and allergen claims are the main risks. Add a "misinformation / unsafe practice" report category, and keep medical or diet claims out of Cooking. No dedicated research was found on this (see Gaps).
- **Art and photography policy.** Decide on and publish an AI policy before launch: options are to ban AI-generated work, or to allow it with a mandatory "AI" label and a separate filter. Prohibit reposting others' work without credit or permission. Optionally offer Glaze or watermark guidance. In art communities, a clear AI stance is both a risk-control and a positioning tool (Cara).
- **EU and UK.** If the beta is US-only, restrict App Store and Play availability to the US to defer UK Online Safety Act and EU DSA/GDPR obligations. Ofcom says even small services must meet illegal-content duties once they serve UK users.

### Gaps
- I found no specific research or policy precedent on food-safety misinformation moderation on cooking or recipe platforms.
- State-level minors' social media laws, app-store age-verification laws, and Apple and Google age-signal APIs were not researched.
- I did not confirm the current DMCA fee on copyright.gov (2016–17 sources).
- I did not research GDPR and the UK GDPR beyond noting their applicability.
- I found no dedicated 2019–2021 empirical study on the effects of hiding like counts.
